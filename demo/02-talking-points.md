# Talking points

What to say at each step. Keep it punchy.

## Opening (30 sec)

- Two workflows do the work: `claude.yml` (interactive `@claude` bot) and `claude-code-review.yml` (auto-review on every PR).
- Both are stock-generated by `/install-github-app`. The interesting parts are the *tuning knobs* — that's what we'll show.
- Repo-side enforcement (ruleset) is the safety net. The prompt is a soft preference; the ruleset is the hard rule.

## Scenario 1 — `@claude` fixes a bug

- The bot is triggered by the `@claude` mention. No human action between filing the issue and getting a PR.
- **Key teaching moment:** out of the box, the bot may commit directly to `main`. We changed that with two layers:
  - Workflow `prompt:` instructs PR-only behavior.
  - Ruleset on `main` makes direct push impossible regardless of what the bot tries.
- Principle: **control plane, not policy memo.**

## Scenario 2 — auto-review

- Two workflows fire: `Tests` (pytest) and `Claude Code Review`.
- Both catch the multiplication bug. Pytest gives the binary signal; Claude posts a prose explanation.
- **Productionization gotcha worth calling out:** the `/code-review` plugin needs `--comment` in the prompt to actually post findings. Without it, Claude runs the full analysis (token cost and all) and silently discards the output. A stock `/install-github-app` workflow doesn't include the flag — easy to miss until you wonder why the bot is quiet.
- **Lesson:** AI review and tests are *defense in depth*, not substitutes. Tests catch the failure; Claude explains the why and points at the specific lines. Both signals are cheap relative to a missed bug in production.
- Reinforces the recurring demo theme: prompts and config are soft preferences. The plugin flag is config; without it the soft layer is missing entirely.

## Scenario 3 — `allowed_bots`

- The action's bot guard exists for two reasons:
  - Prevents bot-on-bot review loops (compute waste, credit burn).
  - Reduces attack surface — a compromised bot account can't farm reviews to exfiltrate context.
- Explicit allowlist (`'claude'`) beats `'*'` for production. Principle of least privilege.

## Scenario 4 — ruleset enforcement

- Why a ruleset and not just the workflow prompt?
  - The prompt is just a string the model usually obeys.
  - If the model is jailbroken, prompt-injected, or future-buggy, the policy still holds.
- Admins bypass — that's how `reset.sh` works. Bots and contributors do not.
- Free-tier note: rulesets only on public repos (without GitHub Pro). For internal MB use that means GHE / Pro tier.

## Bonus security beat — public repo + `@claude` trigger

- The `CLAUDE_CODE_OAUTH_TOKEN` secret is encrypted at rest and never echoed in logs — that part is safe.
- But on a public repo, `issue_comment` and `issues` events can be triggered by **anyone on github.com**. Default `@claude` filter is just `contains(body, '@claude')` — trivial to satisfy.
- Real risks: (1) quota burn from spam triggers, (2) prompt-injection exfiltration via base64 trickery, (3) malicious bot-authored PRs hoping for a sloppy merge.
- Fix in this repo: `author_association` filter restricts triggers to `OWNER` / `MEMBER` / `COLLABORATOR`. Strangers' `@claude` mentions silently no-op.
- **Why this matters for MB:** same shape of risk applies to any GHE-hosted bot that listens to comment events. Always lock the trigger surface to known principals before granting it a credentialed action.

## Closing

- Three knobs to remember when productionizing:
  1. **Workflow prompt** — what the bot should do.
  2. **`allowed_bots` / `if:` filters / `author_association`** — who can trigger what.
  3. **Branch ruleset** — the actual guarantee.
- All three together are what turn a demo into something you can run in a real org.
